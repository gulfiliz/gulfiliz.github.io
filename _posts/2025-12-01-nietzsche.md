In 1882, a madman ran into a marketplace.

It was broad daylight.
He was carrying a lantern.

He cried: *"I seek God! I seek God!"*

The people around him laughed.
Has God got lost? Did he go on a voyage?

The madman turned on them:

*"Where is God? I will tell you.
We have killed him — you and I.
All of us are his murderers.
But how did we do this?
How could we drink up the sea?
Who gave us the sponge to wipe away the entire horizon?
What were we doing when we unchained this earth from its sun?
Whither is it moving now?
Whither are we moving?
Away from all suns?
Are we not plunging continually?
Backward, sideward, forward, in all directions?
Is there still any up or down?
Are we not straying as through an infinite nothing?
Do we not feel the breath of empty space?
Has it not become colder?
Is not night continually closing in on us?"*

He paused.

*"I have come too early.
This tremendous event is still on its way.
It has not yet reached the ears of men."*

Friedrich Nietzsche wrote this in 1882.
We are still catching up.

## I. The Most Misread Philosopher

Nietzsche is the most misread philosopher in the Western tradition.

He has been claimed by Nazis, by self-help culture, by nihilists. 
All wrongly.

His sister Elisabeth, an antisemite who married into the German nationalist movement, falsified his letters and sold his name to the Third Reich. The self-help industry strips his aphorisms of context and pastes them onto motivational posters. The nihilists stop at the death of God without reading what came after.

He has been used to justify domination, cruelty, nationalism, and the worship of strength.

He would have been contemptuous of all of it.

He wrote: *"I am not a man, I am dynamite."*

He meant: his philosophy, properly understood, would explode every comfortable assumption, including the comfortable assumptions of those who thought they were his followers.

To read Nietzsche is to be made uncomfortable.
If you are not uncomfortable, you are not reading him.

## II. The Man

Friedrich Wilhelm Nietzsche was born in 1844 in Röcken, Prussia,
the son of a Lutheran pastor who died of brain disease
when Nietzsche was four years old.

He was a prodigy: appointed professor of classical philology 
at the University of Basel at twenty-four,
before he had even completed his doctorate.

He was a close friend of Richard Wagner,
a friendship that shaped his early work
and whose disintegration shaped his mature work.
When Wagner descended into German nationalism and Christian sentiment,
Nietzsche broke with him completely
and wrote some of the most savage criticism
of Wagner's later work ever composed.

He suffered from chronic, debilitating illness throughout his adult life: 
migraines, vision problems, digestive disorders.
He wrote despite constant pain,
often unable to read for more than an hour a day,
dictating to assistants or writing in large script
to spare his eyes.

In 1889, in Turin, he saw a horse being beaten.
He threw his arms around the horse's neck,
collapsed, and never recovered his sanity.

He spent the last eleven years of his life in a state of mental collapse,
first in an asylum, then in the care of his mother and sister.

He died in 1900, aged fifty-five.

The works that changed philosophy 
(*Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, *Beyond Good and Evil*,
*The Genealogy of Morals*, *Twilight of the Idols*,
*The Antichrist*, *Ecce Homo*) 
were all written between 1883 and 1888.

Five years. In chronic pain. Nearly blind.

*"What does not kill me makes me stronger."*

He meant it literally.

## III. God Is Dead

*"God is dead. God remains dead.
And we have killed him."*

This is the most misunderstood statement in modern philosophy.

Nietzsche was not making an atheist argument.
He was not saying: there is no God, therefore God is dead.
He was saying something far more radical and far more frightening.

What has become unbelievable is the belief in God: the Christian God in particular, with the moral framework that depended on it, and the entire structure of values that had organized Western civilization for two thousand years.

It had not been refuted or disproven. It had become *unbelievable.*

The scientific revolution, the Enlightenment, the historical criticism of the Bible: these had not exactly proved God false. They had made it impossible to believe in God in the way people had believed for centuries, with the naive, unreflective certainty that the cosmos had a moral structure and human life a divinely guaranteed purpose, that suffering had meaning and death was not the end.

This is what Nietzsche means by the death of God.

And the consequences, which he saw with terrifying clarity, were not liberation. Not immediately.

The consequences were **nihilism.**

If God is dead, if the moral order that depended on God has collapsed, on what foundation do we build values? What stops the conclusion that nothing matters?

The madman's terror is real.
He is not celebrating.
He is mourning, and warning.

*"The greatest recent event —
that God is dead, that the belief in the Christian God
has become unbelievable —
is already beginning to cast its first shadows over Europe."*

The shadows are nihilism: 
the recognition that the old values have collapsed
before new values have been found.

## IV. The Will to Power

Nietzsche's concept of the **will to power**
(*Wille zur Macht*) is the most misunderstood
concept in his philosophy.

It does not mean the desire to dominate others, political power, violence, or conquest.

The will to power is Nietzsche's account
of the fundamental drive of all life:
the drive toward **self-overcoming**,
toward growth, toward the expansion of one's capacities.

*"What is good? — All that heightens the feeling of power,
the will to power, power itself in man.
What is bad? — All that proceeds from weakness."*

But power here means the power to create, overcome, grow, not the power to destroy or dominate.

The highest expression of the will to power is the **artist**, the creator, who does not dominate others but overcomes themselves, who takes the raw material of experience and shapes it into something new.

The **Übermensch** (the Overman, the Superman) is not a master race. It is the human being who has overcome herd morality, resentment, pettiness: someone who creates new values rather than inheriting old ones, and who affirms life fully and without reservation.

Nietzsche's examples of people approaching the Übermensch:
Goethe. Napoleon. Caesar.
Not because of their power over others,
but because of their power over themselves,
their ability to contain and transform
suffering and contradiction
into creative force.

## V. Master and Slave Morality

In *The Genealogy of Morals* (1887),
Nietzsche proposed one of the most provocative
accounts of the origins of morality ever written.

He distinguished between two fundamentally different
types of moral evaluation:

**Master morality.**
The strong, the noble, the powerful
define good as what they are:
vital, active, creative, proud.
They define bad as what they are not:
weak, passive, reactive, base.
The evaluation starts with the self.
*"I am good. You are bad."*

**Slave morality.**
The weak, the oppressed, the resentful
cannot overcome the strong directly.
So they revalue values.
They define good as what they are:
humble, meek, suffering, self-denying.
They define evil as what the strong are:
powerful, proud, vital.
The evaluation starts with the other.
*"You are evil. Therefore I am good."*

Slave morality is born from **resentment**, *ressentiment*: 
the festering of impotent hatred
into a moral system that makes a virtue of powerlessness.

Nietzsche's claim, provocative and controversial,
is that Christianity is the great vehicle of slave morality.

The beatitudes (blessed are the meek, the poor in spirit, the suffering) are, Nietzsche argues, the revenge of the weak on the strong, the transformation of powerlessness into a cosmic virtue.

*"Christianity is Platonism for the people."*

This is not mere contempt.
Nietzsche had enormous respect for the psychological sophistication of the Christian tradition: 
its mastery of the inner life,
its understanding of suffering.

His argument is more precise:
that the Christian moral framework,
built on resentment and the denial of life,
is incompatible with the full affirmation of existence
that he believed was necessary.

## VI. Eternal Recurrence

Nietzsche considered the thought of
**eternal recurrence** his most important idea.

The thought experiment:

What if this life, this exact life, with all its suffering, joy, pettiness, and greatness, had to be lived again, infinitely?

Not a similar life. This life: pain, mistakes, losses, moments of grace, all of it repeated exactly, forever.

Could you affirm that?

Could you say *yes, again,* with joy rather than resignation, not because everything was perfect but because you loved it enough to want it eternally?

This is not a cosmological claim.
Nietzsche is not saying the universe actually recurs.

It is a **test**: the most demanding possible test
of one's relationship to life.

If you live in a way that, faced with eternal recurrence, you would say *yes,* then you are living well.

If you would recoil, if the thought of reliving your life fills you with horror, then you are not living as you should.

*"My formula for greatness in a human being
is amor fati: that one wants nothing to be different,
not forward, not backward, not in all eternity.
Not merely bear what is necessary,
still less conceal it,
but love it."*

**Amor fati:** love of fate.

Stronger than acceptance, different from resignation.

The full, unconditional affirmation of everything that has happened and will happen, not because it is all good but because it is all yours, and you would not be without it.

## VII. Apollonian and Dionysian

In his first book, *The Birth of Tragedy* (1872),
written at twenty-seven,
Nietzsche introduced a distinction
that has shaped aesthetics ever since.

**The Apollonian.**
Order, reason, form, clarity, individuation.
The principle of the beautiful illusion:
the dream, the image, the individual.
Named for Apollo, god of light, reason, prophecy.

**The Dionysian.**
Chaos, ecstasy, dissolution, the primal unity.
The shattering of the individual
into collective, ecstatic experience.
Named for Dionysus, god of wine, frenzy, transformation.

Great tragedy (Greek tragedy) holds them both together.
The Dionysian chaos is given Apollonian form.
The Apollonian dream is penetrated by Dionysian truth.

Socrates, Nietzsche argued, killed tragedy
by insisting that reason could explain everything,
that the Dionysian darkness was merely ignorance
to be dissolved by argument.

The Socratic optimism (the belief that
knowledge leads to virtue, that reason leads to happiness)
was, for Nietzsche, a symptom of cultural decline:
the replacement of tragic wisdom
(the full acknowledgment of suffering and darkness)
with shallow rationalism
(the belief that everything can be fixed by thinking harder).

He later revised this view, 
acknowledged that his own early Romanticism had distorted it.
But the distinction between the Apollonian and Dionysian
remains one of the most generative ideas
in the philosophy of art.

## VIII. Perspectivism

One of Nietzsche's most important epistemological contributions
is **perspectivism.**

There is no view from nowhere.
There is no God's-eye perspective,
no neutral, objective standpoint
from which reality can be seen as it truly is.

Every claim to knowledge is made from a perspective,
shaped by the knower's drives, interests, history, embodiment, culture.

*"There are no facts, only interpretations."*

This is not relativism in the simple sense.
It is not the claim that all perspectives are equally valid.
Nietzsche was fiercely evaluative: some perspectives are richer, more encompassing, more life-affirming than others.

But the claim to a perspective-independent truth, the claim that one's own view is simply objective, is self-deception.

The honest thinker acknowledges their perspective,
takes responsibility for it,
and does not hide behind the fiction of objectivity.

This has been enormously influential across philosophy of science, literary theory, feminist epistemology, the sociology of knowledge.

The insight that knowledge is situated, that the knower is never absent from what is known: this is Nietzsche's epistemological legacy.

## IX. He Did Not Comfort Me

I read Nietzsche the way I read Dostoevsky. Not to be reassured. To be caught.

I first read him as a teenager. That was the problem. I have come to think Nietzsche is something like a third-year course in a major. Like quantum mechanics in physics, or data structures or algorithms in computer science. Without the scaffolding to prepare you, the reading effort comes to nothing. Mine did. I read him too early, and what I read stayed half-finished. I would have been ready, perhaps, as a third-year philosophy student at İstanbul University. Even then I think I would have struggled.

I read him from the Hasan Âli Yücel klasikleri series. The translations were beautiful, careful, alive in Turkish.

A few lines have stayed with me ever since.

*"He who has a why to live for can bear almost any how."* From *Twilight of the Idols.*

*"Insanity in individuals is something rare. But in groups, parties, nations and epochs, it is the rule."* From *Beyond Good and Evil,* which is the book of his I love most.

And a line that travels under his name, whether or not it sits in any of his published books: *"And those who were seen dancing were thought to be insane by those who could not hear the music."* I read this as a child and recognized myself. When my family went to weddings I used to watch the dancing adults and think, *Filiz, if there were no music, how funny they would all look.* I had no name for what I was noticing. Years later I had one.

He is not polite or safe or academic. He arrives to shatter the lies you tell yourself.

Three things drew me first.

First, he does not forgive false morality. What people present as goodness, humility, virtue, sacrifice: sometimes underneath these big words, what Nietzsche finds is envy, fear, resentment, the wish to look better than one is. I have always loved that question. *What is really under the big words?* Nietzsche strips people naked. He strips you naked too, if you read honestly.

Second, his idea of self-overcoming. Not the cheap *"be yourself"* of the self-help shelf. Something harder: to create yourself, educate yourself, produce your own values rather than inheriting them. This connects, for me, to Martin Eden. I have always lived close to the conviction that I will not settle for the life given to me. I will construct myself.

Third, the prose. Nietzsche does not write like a textbook. The sentences are knives. The aphorisms are sharp. Sometimes poetry, sometimes insult. I love thinkers who are intellectual, literary, and dangerous all at once.

But I would not fully surrender to him.

What I object to most is that he sometimes belittles compassion too much. I admire strong, self-creating people, people of great mental ambition. But I cannot give myself entirely to a purely ruthless idea of "power." Because inside me there is also the side of my grandmother, my *anane*: protective, attentive, taking human fragility seriously, refusing to look away from what is broken. What Nietzsche calls "weakness" in some places, I would call simply being human.

The aristocratic tone bothers me too. *"I am not for everyone."* *"Übermensch."* *"Herd morality."* Aesthetically these are seductive, intellectually they have purchase. But ethically they are dangerous, and Nietzsche himself did not control how dangerous they would become. I can grant that not everyone is deep or honest or thinking without using that observation to legitimize crushing anyone.

What I love in him is that he exposes the people who invent morality to avoid looking weak. What I refuse is the reading where fragility is always lower, always something to overcome rather than something to honor.

So let me say it as I would say it:

I loved Nietzsche because he did not comfort me. He caught me in the act. I would not surrender to him completely, though, because sometimes the greatest human nobility is not strength. It is staying breakable and refusing to become ugly.

## X. What Remains

The world Nietzsche described is more ours than his.

The moral certainties he watched collapsing have not been rebuilt. The naive religious answer is unavailable to most intellectually serious people. The naive rationalist answer (reason will provide) has not delivered. The questions about value, meaning, and what we owe each other remain open in the way he said they would.

He did not solve this. He named it. That is not nothing.

The diagnosis of resentment also remains. The pattern Nietzsche identified is still recognizable everywhere: making weakness into virtue, envy into morality, inability into religion. He saw it clearly, before most people knew what they were looking at.

And the test of eternal recurrence still works. Not as a cosmological claim. As a test. Faced with reliving your life exactly, in all its suffering and joy, all its mistakes and graces, could you say yes? Not because everything was good. Because it was yours, and you would not be without it.

If you could say yes, you are living well.

If the thought makes you recoil, something is wrong, and it is worth knowing where.

This is the demand of *amor fati.*

Nietzsche made that demand of himself in his last clear years, in chronic pain, nearly blind, writing the books that would survive him. He answered yes by the act of writing them. The answer cost him everything, possibly even his sanity.

But he did answer.

That is what remains.

## Coda

*"I know my fate.
One day my name will be associated
with the memory of something tremendous —
a crisis without equal on earth,
the most profound collision of conscience,
a decision that was conjured up against everything
that had been believed, demanded, hallowed so far.
I am no man. I am dynamite."*

He was right that his name would be associated
with something tremendous.

He could not have predicted what.

The uses of Nietzsche (the misuses, the distortions, the appropriations) are themselves a kind of testament to what he identified: the desperate human need for new values after the death of the old ones, and the danger of filling that void with the first strong voice that comes along.

He was dynamite.
He did not control the explosion.

None of us does.
