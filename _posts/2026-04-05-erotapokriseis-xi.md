**1) Q:** Why does the renormalization group feel less like a technique in physics and more like a way of thinking?  [renormalization group]({% post_url 2026-04-09-renormalization-group %})

**A:** Because it isn't a tool you reach for; it's a lens you can't put down. A technique answers one question. RG tells you which questions even survive once you step back far enough to see the shape of a thing. After a while you start asking it of everything, not just physics: what stays true when I stop looking so closely? That is not a method. That is a way of seeing.

---

**2) Q:** When the scale of something changes, does its truth change as well?

**A:** The parameters do. The electric charge you measure literally depends on how closely you look. But I'd resist saying truth changes; what changes is which description is the honest one at that scale. The world isn't lying to you at low resolution. It's telling you a coarser truth, and RG is the grammar that connects the coarse one to the fine one.

---

**3) Q:** Does RG suggest that the world is understood not through details themselves, but through the way details are forgotten?

**A:** Yes, and that's the part that gives me chills. RG makes forgetting rigorous: it tells you exactly which details you are allowed to throw away, and then proves the answer doesn't depend on them. We spend our lives thinking knowledge means holding on to more. RG says the deepest understanding is knowing precisely what to let go of! 

---

**4) Q:** Why does the idea of universality affect you so much: because order emerges from chaos, or because different systems seem to speak the same language?

**A:** The second, and it isn't close. Order from chaos is beautiful, but I've made my peace with it. What undoes me is that a lump of iron and a jar of fluid near their critical points obey the exact same numbers, having never met, sharing nothing underneath. It's the universe rhyming with itself across systems that have no business sounding alike. Universality is the closest physics comes to admitting reality has only a few real ideas and endless disguises. I will write a blog post on Chaos very soon! Tbh, you inspired me to write one! Cheers. :)

**7) Q:** When we try to protect a quantum computer, what are we really protecting: computation, information, or possibility?

**A:** All three, but most fundamentally, information.

Computation is what we want to do with it; possibility is the strange quantum form it takes before measurement. But what we are really protecting is fragile information from leaking into the world too soon.

A quantum computer is powerful because it can hold possibilities coherently. Error correction is our way of saying: not yet, universe. Do not turn this into history before the computation is done. :)

---

**8) Q:** Is decoherence only a physical problem, or can it also be seen as the universe’s resistance to preserving memory?

**A:** Physically, decoherence is not the universe “resisting” anything; it is the environment entangling with the system and carrying information about it away.

But romanticizing is free, right? :) So, poetically: yes. It feels like the universe reminding us that memory is not free, and that every preserved possibility has to be protected from a world constantly trying to read it. Cheers!

This is almost the same question as Q1, asked by two different people. I tried to answer it the way the reader asked:

**9) Q:** Why does the renormalization group feel less like a physics technique and more like a worldview?

**A:** Because the renormalization group teaches you that not every detail deserves to survive a change of scale.

Some things are relevant, some are irrelevant, and some only look important because you are standing too close. That feels less like a technical lesson in physics and more like a way of thinking about almost everything: ideas, people, pain, memory, even the self.

What remains true after you zoom out? That is the question. :)
