Erotapokriseis XII

Created on June 24, 2026

2026   ·   erotapokriseis,

EN:

Hello, my dear readers, I’ve responded to most of your emails, but I thought I’d answer a few of your questions here as well. Thank you so much for reading and for your thoughtful feedback. It means a lot to me.

TR:

Merhaba sevgili okurlarım, Çoğunuzun e-postasına dönüş yaptım, ancak gelen bazı soruları burada da yanıtlamak istedim. Yazılarımı okuduğunuz ve değerli geri bildirimlerinizi paylaştığınız için çok teşekkür ederim. Hepsi benim için çok kıymetli.

1) Q: Does the apparent fine-tuning of the universe (The Fine-Tuned Universe) fill you with wonder, or with suspicion?

A: Suspicion, mostly. In physics, whenever a number looks suspiciously tuned, it often means we are missing the deeper theory that makes it inevitable, not that someone set a dial. Wonder at the universe always; but “fine-tuned” is usually nature telling me my equations aren’t finished yet.

2) Q: If the constants of the universe could have been otherwise (The Fine-Tuned Universe), does that weaken our idea of reality?

A: Not really, because we don’t actually know they could have been otherwise. We have no complete theory of where the constants come from, and ignorance isn’t the same as contingency. Reality isn’t weakened by our not reaching the bottom; it’s just still holding something back.

3) Q: If we are constantly changing (The Problem of the Self), in what sense are we still the same person?

A: In no fixed sense, and I’ve made peace with that. There’s no little core “me” sitting untouched beneath the changes, only a continuity, a thread of memory and pattern that keeps re-tying itself. I’m not the same person. I’m the same story, still being told.

4) Q: Sometimes understanding requires simplification; when does simplification begin to lie?

A: The moment you forget it was a simplification. A good model tells you where it breaks; a lie is a model that has stopped admitting its own domain. Simplification lies exactly when it starts pretending to be the whole thing.

5) Q: When scale changes (The Renormalization Group), do only the equations change, or does the language of reality change too?

A: Both, and that’s the deep part. The couplings run with scale, yes, but more radically the right degrees of freedom change: quarks and gluons give way to protons, protons to nuclei, and the honest language differs at each level. Renormalization is the statement that reality speaks a different dialect at every scale, and each dialect is honest within its own domain.

6) Q: When we try to manage time (What Is Time?), are we really trying to manage our fear of death?

A: Often, yes. Most of our frantic scheduling is a way of pretending we have an infinite supply of the one thing we don’t. But I’d rather let the fear make the time precious than let it make me busy; those are very different responses to the same clock.

7) Q: Do you find Faust understandable? At what point does the desire to know more become something tragic?

A: Completely understandable; the wanting-to-know is the least alien thing in the world to me. It turns tragic the moment knowing becomes a way of not living, when you would trade every actual morning for one more secret. Faust’s sin isn’t curiosity. It’s contempt for the ordinary hours.

8) Q: As someone who plays Go, do you think the game reveals a person’s intelligence more, or their character?

A: Character, without question. Intelligence gets you through the opening, but the middlegame shows how you handle a losing position, whether you overreach when you’re ahead, whether you can sit calmly inside a mess. You can’t hide who you are over a full board; the stones keep a very honest record.

9) Q: Does symmetry breaking feel to you like the loss of order, or like the emergence of reality?

A: Emergence, every time. The symmetry is still there in the laws; the world simply has to pick one concrete solution in order to exist, and in the picking, structure appears: direction, mass, a magnet’s north. Order isn’t lost when symmetry breaks. That is the exact moment a featureless possibility becomes a real thing.

10) Q: If perfect symmetry is an unseen possibility, is the world we live in real because it is imperfect?

A: Yes, and I find that almost tender. Perfect symmetry is sameness in every direction, which is another way of saying nothing has happened yet; it’s potential, not existence. The world we live in is the broken one, and the breaking is what let anything take shape at all.

11) Q: Did the idea that gravity is not a force but the geometry of spacetime (General Relativity) change your intuition about reality?

A: Profoundly. Once you absorb that a planet orbiting the sun is just following the straightest line it can through curved spacetime, “force” stops being fundamental and becomes a story we told before we saw the shape of things. Nothing pulls the Earth; it goes straight, and the straight is bent.

12) Q: What moves you most about general relativity: its mathematical beauty, or the way it almost turns the world inside out?

A: The turning-inside-out, though the beauty is how it does it. What moves me is that Einstein didn’t add a force; he changed what space and time are, and the equations came out simpler for it. It’s the rare case where the more radical idea is also the more elegant one, and reality sided with the radical.
