Plato — The Cave, the Sun, and the Man Who Chose to Die

Created on December 13, 2024

2024   ·   skepsis,   metaxy,   fragmenta,   philosophy,   EN,

There is a cave.

Inside the cave, prisoners sit chained, facing a wall. Behind them, a fire burns. Between the fire and the prisoners, people walk carrying objects: statues of animals, figures, things.

The shadows of these objects fall on the wall.

The prisoners have been here since birth. They have never seen the fire. They have never seen the objects. They have seen only the shadows.

For them, the shadows are reality. They have names for the shadows. They have expertise in predicting which shadow will appear next. They compete with each other in this expertise. They are proud of what they know.

Then one prisoner is freed.

He turns. He sees the fire, blinding, painful. He is dragged upward, out of the cave, into the sunlight.

He cannot see at first. Everything is too bright, too real, too much. Gradually, he adjusts. He sees the actual objects. He sees the actual world. He sees, finally, the sun itself, the source of all light, all visibility, all truth.

He goes back into the cave.

He tries to tell the others what he has seen.

They do not believe him. His eyes, readjusted to the dark, can no longer predict the shadows as well as they once could.

They think the journey has made him stupid.

If they could, they would kill him.

I. Plato and the Shape of Western Thought

Plato was born in Athens around 428 BCE.

Alfred North Whitehead, the mathematician and philosopher, wrote that the history of Western philosophy is “a series of footnotes to Plato.”

It is not hyperbole, and barely even a metaphor.

Every major question Western philosophy has ever asked, Plato asked first: what is real, what is knowledge, what is justice, what is beauty, what the good life is, how we should organize ourselves, what the relationship is between the particular and the universal. He asked first, and answered with a systematic completeness that has shaped the terms of the debate ever since.

He didn’t just ask questions. He invented the dialogue as a philosophical form: philosophy as conversation, as thinking together rather than handing down conclusions.

He founded the Academy, the first institution of higher learning in the Western world, which ran for over nine hundred years, until the Emperor Justinian closed it in 529 CE.

And he had a teacher whose death shaped everything he wrote.

II. Socrates — The Man Who Knew Nothing

Socrates wrote nothing.

Everything we know about him comes from others: mostly Plato, but also Xenophon and Aristophanes. The Socrates of Plato’s dialogues may or may not be the historical Socrates; scholars have argued about it for centuries.

What is clear: Socrates was a man who walked the streets of Athens asking questions.

Not rhetorical ones, and not questions he secretly had the answers to. Questions he genuinely did not know the answer to, or claimed not to.

He asked generals what courage was. He asked politicians what justice was. He asked poets what beauty was.

In every case, the person he asked began in confidence and ended in confusion, their assumed knowledge dissolved under the patient, relentless pressure of his questioning.

This method, elenchus, refutation, wasn’t meant to humiliate. It was meant to reveal something: that what we think we know, we very often don’t know at all.

The Oracle at Delphi had declared Socrates the wisest man in Athens. Socrates tested the claim by going to talk to people who were assumed to be wise. His conclusion:

“I know that I know nothing, and in this I am wiser than those who think they know but do not.”

This is where philosophy begins: not in the possession of answers, but in the honest confrontation with ignorance.

The Athenians found this intolerable.

III. The Allegory of the Cave — A Close Reading

The Allegory of the Cave appears in Book VII of The Republic, Plato’s most ambitious work, a dialogue about justice that turns into a dialogue about everything.

The cave is not a metaphor for ignorance in the simple sense, for not knowing facts. It is a metaphor for the structure of ordinary consciousness, for the way we all, inevitably, mistake appearance for reality, shadow for substance.

The prisoners are not stupid. They are us.

Their mistake isn’t a failure of intelligence. It is a failure of position. They are chained in a way that keeps them from seeing the sources of what they see. They see effects without causes, appearances without the things that cast them.

The shadows on the wall correspond, in Plato’s framework, to the world of ordinary experience, the world of particular, changing, impermanent things that we take in through the senses.

The objects carried in front of the fire correspond to the mathematical and scientific objects: more real than the shadows, but still dependent on the fire (the intellect) that lights them up.

The sunlit world outside the cave corresponds to the realm of the Forms, the perfect, eternal, unchanging archetypes of which everything in the sensory world is an imperfect copy.

The sun itself corresponds to the Form of the Good, the highest Form, the source of all being and all knowability, which Plato, in some passages, identifies with God.

This is the Theory of Forms, Plato’s most radical and most contested contribution to philosophy.

IV. The Theory of Forms

Consider a circle.

You have seen many circles: drawn on paper, traced in sand, bent out of wire. None of them is a perfect circle. Every drawn circle has its imperfections: slight irregularities, a width to the line, a physical body that falls short of the ideal.

But you know what a perfect circle is. You can recognize that drawn circles approximate it. You can measure how far they fall short.

So where is this perfect circle? It isn’t in the physical world; it has no physical instance anywhere. It isn’t in any single mind either; it isn’t just a private impression.

For Plato, it lives in the realm of Forms, a non-physical, non-mental realm of perfect, eternal objects that the physical world only ever imperfectly copies.

The Form of Circle is more real than any drawn circle, because it is what every drawn circle is trying to be, what makes it a circle and not a squiggle.

Extend this to everything.

The Form of Beauty, which beautiful things imperfectly copy. The Form of Justice, which just acts imperfectly instantiate. The Form of the Good, the highest Form, which lights up all the others the way the sun lights up the physical world.

Mathematical objects, numbers and geometric figures, are the clearest examples of Forms. Seven is not a physical object. It is not in anyone’s head. It is necessarily what it is, eternally, unchangeably, and in Plato’s sense it is more real than any seven apples you could line up on a table.

This is the part I can’t read from a comfortable distance, because I have spent a serious chunk of my life inside a small version of it. I play Go. And anyone who plays seriously knows the feeling: in any given position there is a best move, and it is there whether or not you ever find it. You don’t invent it. You uncover it, sometimes after staring at the board for ten minutes, sometimes not at all. The move was always already the best move. Your blindness to it changes nothing.

So I’ll admit my bias outright: I think mathematics is discovered, not invented. The best move in a position was the best move before anyone ever sat down to play it. That feeling, that you are walking toward something already real rather than building it, is the whole Platonic pull, and I have never once been able to resist it.

The philosopher’s task, the freed prisoner’s task, is to turn away from the shadows and follow the hard path upward: toward knowledge of the Forms, and finally toward the Good itself.

V. Why the Philosopher Must Return

The freed prisoner goes back.

This is the move that separates Plato’s philosopher from the mystic or the pure contemplative.

Having seen the sun, having reached the highest knowledge, the philosopher does not stay up in the light. They go back into the cave. They try to govern, to educate, to lead.

And not because the cave is pleasant. It isn’t, and they will be mocked for coming back, maybe killed for it. They return because justice requires it.

In The Republic, the philosopher-king, the one who has reached knowledge of the Good, is obligated to put that knowledge in the service of the community.

The highest knowledge is not private. It is political. It creates obligation.

This is one of the most contested claims in all of Plato.

Some readers find it authoritarian. Who decides who the philosopher-kings are? Who guards the guardians?

Others find it the only coherent answer to the problem of political legitimacy: rule should be in the hands of those who actually know, not the wealthy or the popular or the well-born, but those who have genuinely understood the Good.

The Republic never resolves the tension. Neither does political philosophy in general. It is one of the permanent problems of the relationship between knowledge and power.

VI. The Death of Socrates

In 399 BCE, Socrates was put on trial.

The charges: impiety (not believing in the gods of the city) and corrupting the youth of Athens.

The trial was political. Socrates had been associated with figures on the losing side of the recent civil conflict, including Critias, one of the brutal Thirty Tyrants.

The jury: 501 Athenian citizens. The vote: 280 to 221 for conviction.

He was given the chance to propose an alternative penalty.

He proposed that Athens should pay him a salary for his service to the city.

The jury, unamused, voted for death: 360 to 141.

He was sentenced to drink hemlock, a poison that causes a slow paralysis, working its way upward from the feet.

He had the chance to escape. His friends arranged it. He refused.

The Crito, one of Plato’s most powerful dialogues, records his reasons.

He had lived his whole life in Athens. He had accepted the benefits of Athenian law. To escape now would be to break the implicit contract between a citizen and the laws of his city, to act as if the laws applied only when convenient, which would dissolve the entire basis of law.

He would not do it.

He drank the hemlock.

The Phaedo records his last hours: a philosophical conversation with his friends about the immortality of the soul.

Socrates argues, with his usual mix of intellectual rigor and personal calm, that the philosopher should not fear death, that the whole philosophical life is a preparation for death, a long practice of separating the soul from the body, of choosing the eternal over the temporary.

His last words, to his friend Crito:

“Crito, we owe a cock to Asclepius. Pay it and do not neglect it.”

Asclepius was the god of healing. A cock was offered to him in gratitude for recovery from illness.

Socrates, dying, was giving thanks: for recovery from the illness of life, for the healing that death brings to a soul that has spent its whole life seeking truth.

Whether you find this beautiful or chilling depends on what you think about the soul.

What’s undeniable is that the man died the way he had lived. Without fear, without pretense, still tending to the small obligations even at the last moment.

VII. What the Cave Gets Right

The Allegory of the Cave is not science. The Theory of Forms is not, in any straightforward sense, true, at least not the way Plato meant it.

But the cave gets something permanently right.

We do not see reality directly.

We see appearances, filtered through our senses, our concepts, our language, the particular architecture of our brains.

The history of science is largely the history of discovering that the obvious is wrong. That the sun does not go around the earth. That solid matter is mostly empty space. That the act of looking changes the thing you look at.

That last one has haunted me since I first really understood quantum mechanics, and it is the cave almost exactly: a century of quantum mechanics spent learning that we never get to see the thing itself, only the shadow it throws when we measure it. (I wrote about that problem on its own elsewhere. It is the cave, with equations.) Plato would have recognized the shape of it on sight.

The cave catches something that naive realism, the idea that we simply see the world as it is, cannot account for.

Education is not the filling of an empty vessel.

Plato’s image of education, the turning of the whole person toward the light, is still the most profound account of what real learning takes.

It isn’t the acquisition of information. It is the reorientation of attention, the slow growth of new ways of seeing, the willingness to be disoriented by something more real than what you were used to.

Knowledge creates obligation.

The philosopher who goes back into the cave, knowledge as the beginning of responsibility rather than an excuse from it, is still one of the strongest arguments against the idea that intellectual life is purely private.

If you see more clearly, you owe something to those who do not yet see.

What you owe, exactly, is the hardest question.

VIII. The Permanent Socratic Question

Socrates did not die for a doctrine, or for the theory of Forms, or for a proof that the soul is immortal.

He died for a practice: the practice of examining life, of refusing to accept unexamined assumptions, of following an argument wherever it leads no matter the social cost.

“The unexamined life is not worth living.”

This sentence, from the Apology, Socrates’s speech at his trial, is the most provocative sentence in Western philosophy.

Provocative not because it is obviously true, but because it is so obviously debatable, and the debate it starts is itself a piece of the examined life it is recommending.

Is the examined life better than the unexamined one? What counts as examination? Is the peasant who never reads a word of philosophy but lives with integrity and love living an unexamined life?

Socrates would have enjoyed these questions. He would have had no final answers.

He would have kept asking.

That, for him, was the point.

IX. Plato’s Shadow on Everything

Plato never really leaves.

Every time you separate appearance from reality, you are doing something Platonic.

Every time you catch yourself believing that mathematics is discovered rather than invented, that the Pythagorean theorem was true before anyone proved it, that the best move in a position was the best move before anyone found it, you are a Platonist about mathematics. (I am, without apology. See above.)

Every time you feel that justice is not simply whatever the powerful decide, that there is a standard the powerful themselves can be measured against, you are reaching for something like the Form of Justice.

Plato isn’t a historical curiosity. He is the shape of certain ways of thinking we still haven’t found a way to abandon, because they catch something true about how we actually deal with truth, with value, with the world.

Even the people who reject him hardest, Aristotle, Nietzsche, the logical positivists, are defined by the rejection. You cannot escape Plato. You only get to choose your relationship to him.

Start with the Apology, Socrates’s speech at his trial.

Then the Phaedo, his last hours :’(.

Then the Republic, if you have the patience for it. It will repay you beyond almost any book I know.

The Allegory of the Cave is Book VII, but read Books I through VI first.

The cave means more once you know what came before it.
