In Defense of Boredom: Why Doing Nothing Is the Most Radical Act Left

Created on June 15, 2024

2024   ·   skepsis,   metaxy,   filizinbetween,

I can’t sit still.

I mean it literally. I am someone who cannot do nothing for twenty seconds. If there is nothing to do, I will invent something. If the house is already clean, I will clean it again. Empty time makes me itch, and then it makes me angry at myself for being idle, which is a special kind of talent.

I didn’t choose this. I inherited it. Boş durma, don’t sit idle, was practically a household law when I was growing up, and I absorbed it so completely that now, decades later, an unused hour feels like a small theft. Time passing without being spent on something makes me anxious. Time is, if I’m honest, my obsession.

The proof is old. When I was a child and my grandmother told me to sit still for a minute, I would lift my toes inside my shoes, one by one, then put them back down. My one secret motion, the one I was sure she couldn’t see. When she caught me, I froze. The second she turned her head, I started again, faster this time, as if I had to win back the seconds I’d lost holding still. I was maybe six. I’m telling you this as a confession, because honestly not much has changed.

So when I say I tried to do nothing yesterday and lasted about twenty seconds before my hand was already in my pocket reaching for something, please understand: this is not a think-piece written from above. It is a field report from inside the problem.

And here is the part that confuses even me. I play Go. Competitively. I will sit at a board for eight hours, stare at a single position for ten minutes doing nothing but thinking, and feel completely calm. The same person who can’t last twenty seconds on a bench. I have been turning that contradiction over for a long time, and I have started to think it is the whole point.

But let me back up.

I. They Took It On Purpose

Boredom is being engineered out of existence, and not by accident.

Every app, every feed, every notification system is built around one goal: making sure you never have an empty second.

The infinite scroll exists so the feed never ends, so that the moment you hit the bottom and boredom might creep in, more appears.

Autoplay exists so the gap between episodes, that little pause where you might decide to stop, never happens.

Notifications are timed to catch you exactly when your attention starts to drift, and reel it back.

None of this is a side effect. It is the product.

The whole business model depends on swallowing your empty time, because every bored moment is a moment you are not scrolling, not watching, not generating the attention these companies sell. Boredom is bad for business. So boredom has to go.

People like me, by the way, were the easiest customers they ever had. I came pre-trained to hate the empty second. They barely had to show up.

II. What Boredom Actually Is

It helps to be precise about what we’re defending, because boredom is not the same as emptiness.

Boredom is the gap between how much stimulation your mind wants and how little the room is actually giving it. It is restlessness, dissatisfaction, the itch of a mind hunting for something to do and finding nothing good enough. (I know this one intimately. See above.)

It is unpleasant. That is exactly the point.

That discomfort is a signal. The mind is telling you it has run out of things to receive and now has to make something instead.

And if you are bored, genuinely, uncomfortably bored, and you do not reach for your phone, something does start to happen.

The mind begins to move on its own. It wanders back to problems you’d shelved. It makes connections you weren’t trying to make. It produces images and ideas out of its own materials instead of waiting to be fed.

This isn’t mystical. It’s just what an unoccupied brain does when you finally leave it alone.

There’s even a name for the machinery behind it. The default mode network: the set of brain regions that light up when you are not focused on some external task.

It’s associated with things like:

  • self-referential thought
  • seeing things from someone else’s perspective
  • creative ideation
  • thinking about the future
  • pulling your experiences together into a story

It is most active when you are doing nothing in particular. When you are bored.

So when we fill every gap with input, we are quietly switching it off, and we stop integrating, stop imagining, stop making our own meaning. We become consumers of meaning instead of producers of it.

III. A Surprisingly Young Word

Here is something that surprised me: “boredom” as a word is barely older than the railroad. It first shows up in English around the 1850s, right alongside the rush of industrial life.

Before that, most lives ran on seasonal time, communal time, religious time. There was hard labor and there was rest, and the rest wasn’t really boredom in our sense. It was structured emptiness, filled with talk, with stories, with slow work done by hand.

Industrial time invented a new thing: hours that were neither work nor real rest, time that simply had to be filled, time whose emptiness felt vaguely threatening.

The Victorians were genuinely anxious about idleness. The devil makes work for idle hands. Empty time as a moral hazard.

I recognized that immediately, because I grew up with its descendant. Boş durma is the same instinct, handed down through a Turkish grandmother instead of a Victorian pulpit. Idleness is suspect, time must be used, sitting still is something you have to justify. The terror never went away. It just got passed around, and eventually it got monetized.

The attention economy didn’t invent our discomfort with empty time. It inherited it, the way I did, and then made it far worse than any Victorian could have dreamed.

IV. Pascal Would Have Diagnosed Me on Sight

In the 17th century, Blaise Pascal wrote the line that has been following me around for years:

“All of humanity’s problems stem from man’s inability to sit quietly in a room alone.”

The first time I read it I laughed, and then I felt caught. Because that is me. That is the toe-wiggling six-year-old, just grown up.

Pascal’s point was that we cannot stand to be alone with ourselves, so we run: into distraction, into company, into endless activity. He called it divertissement, diversion. Not entertainment in the harmless sense. Flight.

And we flee, he thought, because of what is waiting when we finally stop: our own mortality, the smallness of everything we cling to, the question of who we even are when we are not performing or producing or consuming.

I don’t have to share his answer to that question (his was God) to admit he nailed the diagnosis. We cannot sit quietly in a room alone.

And here is the thing I have only recently understood: it isn’t because there is nothing in that quiet room. It is because there is too much.

V. The Cure That Makes You Sicker

This is where Baudrillard comes in, whom I’ve been reading lately.

He described the hyperreal: a world where the simulation has quietly replaced the real thing.

And one job the hyperreal does, maybe its main job, is to manage our boredom.

The simulation is always more exciting than the room you are actually in. The show is more dramatic than your day. The feed is more interesting than your ceiling. The game is more stimulating than just existing.

So the hyperreal “cures” boredom by handing you a version of reality that has been engineered to be more vivid than reality, tuned precisely to the reward circuits in your brain.

The trouble is that the cure is the disease.

Every time you reach for the phone when the silence starts to creep in, you take the cure, and you teach yourself to need it again.

You get a little less able to sit with the emptiness, a little more dependent on the simulation, a little worse at making your own thoughts.

The attention economy isn’t just selling you distraction. It is slowly removing your ability to live without it.

VI. The Part With the Studies

The research here is getting harder to wave away. A few that stuck with me:

Attention restoration theory (Kaplan, 1995). You have two kinds of attention. The directed kind, focused and effortful, is a limited tank that drains. The involuntary kind, the soft diffuse awareness you get in a forest or staring at water, is what refills it.

Boredom in a natural, unstructured setting flips you from the first into the second, and that is restorative. Boredom medicated by a phone flips you nowhere. It just keeps you in effortful mode on a cheaper stimulus, so the tank never refills.

Mind-wandering (Smallwood & Schooler, 2015). The loose, undirected thinking that happens when you are bored turns out to correlate with better creative problem-solving. The wandering mind makes the strange connections. The mind that is always pointed at a screen can’t wander, so it doesn’t.

The phone on the desk (Ward et al., 2017). People did cognitive tasks with their phone either face-down on the desk, in a pocket, or in another room. They performed measurably better with it in another room. Face-down and silent wasn’t enough. Just knowing it was there ate part of their minds.

Now, here is my own complication. By all of this I should be a cognitive disaster, and in everyday life maybe I am. But I play Go, and at a board I can sit perfectly still inside a single position for ten minutes, thinking, and feel nothing but calm. Eight hours of it. The same person who can’t manage twenty seconds on a bench.

For a long time that looked like a contradiction. I don’t think it is anymore. Go isn’t doing nothing. It is stillness with walls around it, stillness pointed at something.

And it turns out I can hold still almost indefinitely as long as the stillness has a job.

That both comforts me and worries me. It comforts me because it means the capacity is in there somewhere. It worries me because the thing I still can’t do is the other kind, the open kind, the stillness with no walls and no task. The kind this whole essay is about.

VII. What People Make in the Empty Spaces

Let me be concrete about what the empty time is actually for.

Newton. When the plague closed Cambridge in 1665, he went home to his mother’s farm at Woolsthorpe and spent about eighteen months with no colleagues, no students, none of the usual machinery of a working life.

In that quiet he worked out calculus, the law of universal gravitation, and the foundations of optics.

Arguably the most productive year and a half in the history of science, and it happened in something very close to enforced boredom.

Darwin. His method, by his own account, was walking. He had a gravel loop at Down House he called the Sandwalk, his “thinking path,” and he would circle it doing nothing but turning problems over. Not reading, not writing. Walking and thinking. He counted it among his most productive hours.

Proust. In Search of Lost Time, seven volumes, the longest novel in the Western tradition, was written by a man in a cork-lined room, mostly in bed, with almost nothing coming in from outside.

The whole engine of the book, the involuntary memory, the madeleine in the tea, the uneven paving stones, is basically a theory of what an unstimulated mind does when you leave it alone.

It goes backward. It recovers. It finds, in the empty moment, the full weight of everything already lived.

And that recovery needs silence. It needs the absence of input. It needs, precisely, the thing I keep running away from.

None of which, I’ll admit, is especially comforting to someone whose response to a free afternoon is to go clean a house that is already clean.

VIII. There’s a Political Cost Too

This is the argument I rarely hear anyone make, so let me make it.

The attention economy is not politically neutral.

When every empty moment of yours gets filled with content that has been optimized for engagement and outrage and tribal signal, you are not exactly a free citizen. You are a node in a system whose profits flow to whoever owns the algorithm. Your attention is the product, and your opinions, shaped by whatever you have been shown, are the output.

Real independent thought needs the opposite of all this. It needs the ability to be alone with your own mind long enough to consider something, doubt it, reconsider, and arrive somewhere slowly, instead of reacting in the fast emotional spasms that feeds are built to trigger.

Hannah Arendt wrote about exactly this. She described thinking as a kind of two-in-one dialogue of the self with itself, and treated it as the ground of any moral or political life.

“The sad truth is that most evil is done by people who never make up their minds to be good or evil.”

Her most famous idea, the banality of evil, comes down to thoughtlessness. Not stupidity. Thoughtlessness: the failure to stop, to sit with the question, to be alone for one moment with what you are actually doing.

And the attention economy manufactures that thoughtlessness at industrial scale. It is not neutral.

IX. So I’m Practicing. Badly.

I am not going to tell you to throw away your phone. I am telling you what I am trying to do myself, which is smaller and somehow much harder.

I am practicing being bored. On purpose.

It is not meditation, though it is a cousin. It is not a “digital detox,” though that is a cousin too. It is just scheduled appointments with emptiness.

Sit somewhere. No phone, no book, no music, no podcast keeping me company. Ten minutes, ideally. Twenty seconds, realistically, at least at the start, because remember who you are dealing with.

And when the discomfort shows up, and it shows up fast, the job is not to run from it.

I sit with it. I watch what my mind does with nothing to chew on. I notice the thoughts that arrive without being invited, the problems I have apparently been carrying around without ever giving myself the room to feel them.

I am, I promise you, only at the very beginning of this, and I am bad at it. Trying to do nothing turns out to be full-blown existential dread for me. Genuinely. :D

But every so often, for a few seconds, the panic loosens, and I notice that boredom isn’t the monster I had built it into.

It is just a space. A space where something can happen that cannot happen anywhere else, as long as you can stand to stay in it.

X. The Most Radical Thing Left

In a world built for total stimulation, doing nothing on purpose starts to look like a small act of resistance.

Not a revolution. Not a fix for anything structural. Just a refusal: to be one more node in the attention network, to hand over your empty time, to let your capacity for solitude and daydream and the slow movement of thought quietly waste away because a system profits from its absence.

The Greeks had a word for what waits on the other side of that refusal: ἀταραξία, ataraxia. Usually translated as tranquility, or undisturbedness. Not the absence of thought, but the presence of a self that is gathered and quiet and available to its own deepest resources.

It was something you had to practice. You had to go and meet the emptiness on purpose. You had to be willing, in our terms, to be bored.

Seneca put it plainly: “Retire into yourself as much as you can.”

William James, much later, said the thing I most need to hear: “The faculty of voluntarily bringing back a wandering attention, over and over again, is the very root of judgment, character, and will.”

Over and over again. That is the whole practice. Losing the attention, bringing it back. Losing it, bringing it back.

So here is where I have landed, as a lifelong member of the can’t-sit-still club.

I still catch myself wiggling my toes, metaphorically, in every quiet minute, trying to win back time I haven’t actually lost. Boş durma is still in there, in my grandmother’s voice, and it probably always will be.

But I am learning, slowly and badly, to sit in the empty room anyway. To let the hour go unspent. To do, for a few unbearable and then somehow bearable seconds, absolutely nothing.

Try it with me. Put the phone down. Sit somewhere. Let the boredom come, and don’t flee.

See what it brings.

That’s the assignment.
